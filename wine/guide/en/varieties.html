Japan's Grape Variety Landscape

Japanese wineries grow an extraordinary range of grape varieties — from 1,000-year-old indigenous whites to German aromatic varieties that found their ideal climate in Hokkaido. Understanding the grapes is the key to understanding the wines.

Japan's grape variety mix reflects its winemaking history. The 19th-century pioneers planted whatever would survive Japan's humid summers — indigenous varieties with thicker skins and disease resistance, along with American hybrids (Campbell Early, Delaware) that tolerated conditions impossible for Vitis vinifera. As winemaking knowledge grew through the 20th century, international varieties arrived. The most successful — Chardonnay in Hokkaido, Merlot in Nagano — found microterroirs that genuinely suit them. Today, Japan grows over 50 wine grape varieties commercially, with a handful defining the country's identity.

Two OIV-Recognized Varieties. Of all the grapes grown in Japan, two have been formally registered with the OIV (International Organisation of Vine and Wine) as internationally recognized varieties: Koshu (white, registered 2010) and Muscat Bailey A (red, registered 2013). Registration acknowledges that these grapes represent distinct, stable varieties — the same standard that applies to Cabernet Sauvignon or Chardonnay.

Koshu — The Grape That Defines Japanese Wine

🍇
Koshu (甲州)
甲州 — Yamanashi Prefecture
Indigenous Japanese White Wine OIV Registered 2010
Citrus peel White peach Mineral Subtle umami Gentle bitterness Low alcohol

Koshu is Japan's most important wine grape — the variety that most clearly expresses something unique and unreplicable about Japanese terroir. With over 1,000 years of cultivation history in Yamanashi Prefecture, Koshu has been shaped by centuries of selection into a grape of unusual character: thin-skinned, pinkish-grey in color (partly due to trace amounts of anthocyanin in the skin), and producing wines of exceptional delicacy and restraint.

The grape's defining quality is its relationship with umami. Where European whites derive character primarily from fruit, mineral, and acid, Koshu adds a savory dimension — a subtle, seaweed-like depth that makes it uniquely responsive to Japanese cuisine. This is not accidental: Koshu evolved alongside Japan's dashi culture, its amino acid profile perfectly complementing the glutamates in bonito broth, soy sauce, and fermented foods. Sommeliers in Japan frequently recommend Koshu as the wine that "tastes like Japan itself."

Stylistically, Koshu is made in several formats. The most common is stainless steel-fermented, unoaked, with bracing freshness and delicate fruit. Sur Lie aging (leaving the wine on its lees — spent yeast cells — for several months) adds a creamy texture and a faint brioche note while preserving the grape's mineral character. Orange Koshu (skin-contact fermentation, sometimes called "ramune Koshu") is a growing category: the skin contact extracts phenolic compounds that deepen color, add texture, and amplify the wine's savory, umami-rich quality. Several Yamanashi producers also make Pétillant Naturel (pet-nat) Koshu — a lightly sparkling, cloudy style produced by bottling before fermentation is complete, trapping natural CO2.

Notable Koshu producers: Grace Wine (Beau Michelle Koshu, widely considered the benchmark), Lumière (Hikari Koshu, organically farmed), Château Mercian (Koshu Gris de Gris — a rosé-style from pinkish skins), Suntory Tomi no Oka Winery, Marquis Winery (natural-style Koshu). Annual production: approximately 3,000 tonnes of Koshu grapes in Yamanashi. The variety is also grown in small quantities in Nagano, Shizuoka, and Osaka.

Muscat Bailey A — Japan's Red Grape Soul

🍇
Muscat Bailey A (マスカット・ベーリーA)
MBA — Nationwide (Yamanashi, Niigata, Nagano)
Indigenous Japanese Hybrid Red Wine OIV Registered 2013
Strawberry candy Raspberry Rose petals Light body Low tannin Foxy character

Muscat Bailey A is the grape that Zenbei Kawakami bet Japan's red wine future on — and he was right. In 1927, at the Iwanohara Vineyard in Niigata Prefecture, Kawakami crossed Bailey (itself a cross of Triumph and Muscat Hamburg) with Muscat Hamburg to create a red grape capable of thriving in Japan's difficult climate. The result was a disease-resistant, early-ripening variety with natural resistance to humidity, spring frost, and the diseases that plague European Vitis vinifera in Japanese conditions.

Muscat Bailey A — typically abbreviated "MBA" in Japan — is now the country's most widely planted red wine grape. Its wines are immediately recognizable: light ruby to medium ruby in color, with an intensely perfumed aroma of strawberry candy (what wine scientists call the "foxy" character of North American hybrid grapes, derived from the compound methyl anthranilate). The palate is light-bodied with soft, almost silky tannins and a finish of fresh red fruit. At its best — from older vines in Yamanashi or Nagano — MBA produces wines of genuine complexity: earthy, savory, with a depth that belies its reputation as a simple drink.

The most sophisticated Muscat Bailey A producers age their wines in oak, which tames the foxy character and adds a savory, spice-box dimension. Château Mercian and Grace Wine both produce oak-aged MBA of international standing. The variety's food-friendliness is legendary — its light tannins and high natural acidity make it ideal with grilled chicken, pork belly, teriyaki dishes, and the full range of Japanese izakaya food.

Delaware — The People's Grape

🍇
Delaware (デラウェア)
デラウェア — Niigata, Yamanashi
North American Hybrid White / Rosé Wine
Muscatel Lychee Off-dry Light Refreshing

Delaware is an American hybrid grape (Vitis labrusca origin) that arrived in Japan as a table grape in the late 19th century and gradually became one of the country's most significant wine grapes. In Niigata Prefecture — where rice paddy fields occasionally give way to grape trellises — Delaware is the dominant variety, producing light, aromatic, gently sweet wines that are consumed locally at izakayas and festivals. The variety's natural disease resistance and reliable yields make it economically important for small-scale farmers.

Delaware wine is rarely exported, and outside Japan it is largely unknown. But within Japan, it represents an authentic piece of wine culture: accessible, affordable, and deeply rooted in local agricultural tradition. Some Niigata producers are now making sparkling Delaware of real quality — the grape's floral aromatics and natural acidity make it well-suited to méthode traditionnelle sparkling wine.

Kerner — Hokkaido's Signature White

❄
Kerner (ケルナー)
ケルナー — Hokkaido (Furano, Yoichi)
German Origin White Wine
White peach Muscat Fresh acidity Floral Crisp finish

Kerner is a German cross (Trollinger × Riesling) that found its Japanese home in Hokkaido's cool northern vineyards. Developed in 1929 at the German Weinsberg Research Institute, Kerner shares Riesling's tendency toward fresh acidity, aromatic precision, and the capacity to reflect its growing site — qualities that translate exceptionally well to Hokkaido's cool, relatively dry summers. Hokkaido Wine Co. built much of its commercial success on Kerner, and the grape remains central to the island's white wine identity.

At its best, Hokkaido Kerner offers a Riesling-like aromatic lift — white peach, dried apricot, subtle floral notes — with a clean, mineral finish. Some producers are experimenting with late-harvest Kerner for dessert wines; the grape's high natural acidity balances sweetness beautifully.

Dewa33 — Yamagata's Own Variety

🌳
Dewa33 (出羽33)
出羽33 — Yamagata Prefecture
Yamagata Local Hybrid Red Wine
Wild berries Spice Vivid fruit Medium body

Dewa33 is a red wine grape developed by Yamagata Prefecture's agricultural research station as a cross between Muscat Bailey A and Campbell Early. The "33" refers to Showa 33 (1958), the year of its development. It was created specifically to thrive in Yamagata's continental climate — cold winters, hot summers, and the risk of spring frost. The variety is earlier-ripening than Muscat Bailey A and more resistant to cold, making it a reliable choice for Yamagata's demanding conditions.

Wines from Dewa33 tend to be medium-bodied with vivid red fruit, more structural tannin than MBA, and a spicy, earthy finish. The variety is primarily grown in Yamagata and rarely seen in other prefectures. As GI Yamagata matures, Dewa33 may become the variety most closely associated with Yamagata's regional identity — much as Koshu defines Yamanashi.

International Varieties in Japan

Japan's most exciting wines are increasingly coming from international varieties that have found their ideal Japanese expression. Here are the most significant:

Chardonnay in Hokkaido: Cool-climate Hokkaido produces Chardonnay of striking mineral clarity, closer in style to Chablis or Champagne base wine than to oaky Burgundy. The best examples show green apple, sea breeze, and a clean, incisive finish with natural high acidity. Some producers make a deliberately unoaked, low-alcohol style (around 11% ABV) that is beautifully suited to seafood.

Merlot in Nagano: Nagano's Kikyogahara district produces Merlot that has beaten French examples in blind tastings. The high-altitude site, volcanic soils, and large day-night temperature swings create Merlot of unusual depth and longevity — plummy, silky, with a firm backbone that supports 10+ years of aging. Château Mercian's Kikyogahara Merlot (first vintage 1985) remains the benchmark.

Pinot Noir in Hokkaido and Nagano: Japan's Pinot Noir scene is exploding. Yoichi (Hokkaido) produces the country's most celebrated examples — precise, transparent, with a Burgundian delicacy that has attracted the attention of international critics. Domaine Takahiko's "Nou" Pinot Noir from a single 0.4-ha plot has become one of Japan's most-sought wines. Nagano's higher-altitude zones also produce excellent Pinot, slightly fuller-bodied than Hokkaido's.

Zweigelt in Hokkaido: This Austrian variety (Blaufränkisch × St. Laurent) thrives in Hokkaido's cool climate, producing wines of lively acidity, crunchy red fruit, and a distinctive peppery character. Less known internationally than Pinot Noir, Hokkaido Zweigelt is a wine drinker's discovery.

Cabernet Sauvignon and Franc in Yamanashi: Several Yamanashi producers, particularly in the hillside plots above Katsunuma, produce Cabernet Sauvignon and Franc of genuine quality. Château Mercian's Yamanashi Cabernet Sauvignon from the Maruki Winery project shows real Bordeaux-comparable depth.
