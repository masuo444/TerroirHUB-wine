OVERVIEW

The Japanese Winemaking Challenge

Japan presents winemakers with a uniquely difficult set of conditions — and the solutions they have developed are as distinctive as the wines they produce.

Make no mistake: growing wine grapes in Japan is hard. The summer growing season coincides with a monsoon period that delivers abundant rainfall and high humidity — the enemy of thin-skinned Vitis vinifera grapes. Typhoons strike each year between July and October, sometimes devastating harvests. Spring frosts threaten Hokkaido's early-budding varieties. The volcanic soils that dominate much of Japan's wine country are low in nutrients, requiring careful management.

And yet these challenges have produced not just resilient grapes, but distinctively Japanese winemaking philosophies. The need to manage humidity drove the development of the tanashitate pergola trellis system — a practice unique to Japan. The vulnerability of Koshu's thin skins led to extraordinarily gentle whole-cluster pressing techniques. Japan's emphasis on delicacy and restraint over power shapes every decision from harvest timing to aging duration. The result is wine that looks, smells, and tastes like nowhere else.

One key number: Japan's annual production of "Japanese wine" (domestic grapes only) is approximately 20,000–25,000 kiloliters — less than 0.1% of global wine production. Every bottle is, in a sense, a rare thing. Japan imports about 350,000 kiloliters of wine annually, meaning domestic Japanese wine represents roughly 6–7% of total wine consumed in Japan — a quality niche with dedicated domestic consumers and a growing international following.

The 7 Steps of Japanese Winemaking

1
Viticulture
Site Selection & Growing Season

Japan's best wine sites share three characteristics: well-drained soils on slopes that shed rain quickly, good sun exposure without excessive afternoon heat, and prevailing winds that dry the canopy after rainfall. Yamanashi's alluvial fans at the base of the Southern Alps offer all three; so do Nagano's volcanic plateaus and Hokkaido's west-facing coastal slopes.

The traditional tanashitate (棚仕立て) pergola trellis system fans vines horizontally over head height, maximizing airflow through the canopy and reducing botrytis and downy mildew risk in humid summers. It also eases harvesting — workers walk beneath the trellis to pick grapes overhead. Increasingly, producers are switching to vertical shoot positioning (VSP) for easier canopy management and better fruit quality, but tanashitate remains the dominant system in Yamanashi.

Rain covers (ビニールハウス) over vineyards during the ripening period are a uniquely Japanese practice. Plastic sheets over the trellis block rainfall while allowing airflow and sunlight, protecting thin-skinned Koshu grapes from splitting and fungal disease. The technique adds significant labor cost but is essential for quality Koshu production.

2
Harvest
Picking at Exactly the Right Moment

Harvest timing in Japan is a race against the calendar. Koshu in Yamanashi is typically harvested in early to mid-September, before the major typhoon season peaks. Harvesting too late risks splitting after autumn rains and fungal infection; too early means underripe, harsh wines. The best producers monitor Brix (sugar content) and taste grapes daily from late August.

Hokkaido Pinot Noir is harvested in October — a longer hang time than Yamanashi, thanks to the island's cooler temperatures and later summer. The extended ripening allows full phenolic maturity with naturally high acidity, producing wines of real complexity. The risk is early autumn frost, which can arrive as early as mid-October in higher Hokkaido vineyards.

Hand harvesting is standard at quality-focused Japanese producers. The traditional family-farm structure of Yamanashi's vineyards — many plots under 0.3 hectares — makes machine harvesting impractical in any case. Harvest is often a family affair, with extended family and neighbors joining to pick over a few intense days.

3
Pressing
Gentle Extraction for Delicate Wines

Koshu's defining characteristic — delicacy — demands gentle pressing. Most quality Koshu producers use whole-cluster pressing: entire bunches go directly into the press without destemming or crushing. This minimizes skin contact before pressing, extracting juice of crystalline purity with minimal phenolic extraction. The result is a pale, almost colorless juice that ferments into wine of exceptional finesse.

Orange Koshu (skin-contact fermentation) reverses this philosophy deliberately: crushed Koshu with skins is left to macerate for days or weeks before pressing. The skin contact extracts phenolic compounds, anthocyanins (giving the wine a copper-gold color), and the grape's full aromatic potential. The result is more textured, savory, and complex than conventionally pressed Koshu — a wine that can stand up to more richly flavored Japanese dishes.

Red grapes are destemmed and lightly crushed before fermentation. Gentle extractive techniques — shorter maceration times, lower pump-over pressure — preserve fruit character and avoid harsh tannin extraction, particularly important for Muscat Bailey A, which can become coarse if over-extracted.

4
Fermentation
Cool Temperatures & Concrete Innovation

White wine fermentation in Japan typically occurs at 12–16°C in temperature-controlled stainless steel tanks — cool enough to preserve aromatic freshness and delicate ester compounds. This low-temperature approach is particularly important for Koshu, whose subtle aromas can easily be overwhelmed by high fermentation temperatures.

Red wines ferment at 22–28°C, with regular pump-overs or punch-downs to extract color, tannin, and flavor from the grape skins. Fermentation typically lasts 7–14 days for reds, depending on the extraction level sought. Premium producers often ferment in open-top wooden or concrete vats that allow atmospheric influence and more nuanced tannin development.

Concrete eggs are a growing trend among progressive Japanese producers. These ovoid concrete fermentation vessels — popularized by Burgundy's Domaine Leroy in the 1990s — allow the wine to circulate naturally during fermentation (the ovoid shape creates convection currents), producing wines of remarkable textural density without the oak influence of barrel fermentation. Several Yamanashi and Nagano producers have invested in concrete eggs for their top Koshu and Chardonnay cuvées.

5
Aging
Sur Lie, Oak & the Aging Question

Sur Lie aging (leaving wine on its lees — spent yeast cells — after fermentation) is one of the most important techniques in the Koshu winemaker's toolkit. As the dead yeast cells decompose (a process called autolysis), they release complex flavor compounds into the wine: a creamy, brioche-like richness, increased body, and a faint nutty note. The process also naturally protects the wine from oxidation, allowing for more subtle winemaking with less sulfur addition. Koshu Sur Lie wines are aged for three months to over a year on the lees before bottling.

Red wine aging in oak barriques (225-liter French oak barrels) is standard for premium Japanese reds. Nagano Merlot is typically aged for 18–24 months in a mix of new and second-use French oak. Muscat Bailey A is more sensitive to oak — over-oaked examples lose their signature fruit character — so most producers use older barrels or larger vessels (500-liter demi-muids) to provide structure without overwhelming the grape's aromatic freshness.

Japanese oak (Mizunara, 水楢) is an occasional curiosity in wine aging. The same oak species famous for imparting a distinctive sandalwood and coconut character to Japanese whisky can be used for wine barrel aging, though its extreme porosity and the difficulty of coopering it into tight barrels make it a costly specialty. A handful of Yamanashi producers have experimented with Mizunara-aged wines with intriguing results.

6
Blending
Assemblage and Single-Variety Philosophy

Japanese winemakers divide into two philosophical camps on blending. The Yamanashi tradition — inherited from France — values assemblage: blending lots from different vineyard blocks, varieties, or aging vessels to create a balanced final wine. A typical Grace Wine Beau Michelle Koshu might blend multiple lots from different farms and harvesting dates to achieve consistency and complexity.

The opposing philosophy, gaining strength in Hokkaido and Nagano, favors single-vineyard, single-variety wines that express a specific place. Domaine Takahiko's "Nou" Pinot Noir is the paradigmatic example: a single 0.4-hectare plot, no blending, expressing only what that particular place and vintage delivered. This site-expression philosophy mirrors the Burgundy grand cru model and is driving Japan's most internationally acclaimed wines.

7
Bottling
Closures & Finishing Choices

Screwcap (Stelvin) closures are increasingly standard for Japanese white wines, particularly Koshu. The logic is unambiguous: Koshu's delicate aromatics are vulnerable to the cork taint compound TCA (trichloroanisole), and its freshness-driven style does not benefit from the micro-oxidation that natural corks provide. Most forward-thinking Koshu producers made the switch to screwcap in the 2010s, following New Zealand's lead. Traditional cork closures remain standard for premium reds intended for aging.

Pétillant Naturel (pet-nat) wines — bottled before primary fermentation is complete, capturing natural CO2 — are a growing niche in Japan, particularly for Koshu. These cloudy, lightly sparkling wines have a fresh, yeasty character and a gentle fizz; they are typically sealed with crown caps and consumed young. Several natural wine producers in Yamanashi now make annual pet-nat Koshu releases that sell out immediately to domestic enthusiasts.

Natural Wine & Biodynamic Movement in Japan

Japan has developed a vibrant natural wine movement since the 2010s, centered primarily in Yamanashi and Hokkaido. Natural winemaking in Japan generally means: no or minimal sulfur additions, use of wild (indigenous) yeast rather than commercial strains, minimal fining and filtration, and organic or biodynamic viticulture. The results are wines of striking individuality — sometimes rustic, always honest, and deeply reflective of their specific site and vintage.

Producers like Marquis Winery and Haramo Wine in Yamanashi, and Domaine Mont in Hokkaido, have built cult followings with their natural approach. Their wines are sold through subscription lists and selected natural wine bars in Tokyo, rarely reaching supermarket shelves. The domestic natural wine community — a subset of Japan's food-obsessed urban culture — has embraced these producers with the same intensity they bring to natural sake (kimoto and yamahai) and artisan shochu.

Biodynamic viticulture remains rare but is growing. The additional cost and labor of biodynamic farming — which requires adherence to a lunar planting calendar and preparations made from fermented plant and mineral materials — is challenging for small Japanese wineries operating on thin margins. But several producers, including natural wine pioneer Coco Farm & Winery (Tochigi Prefecture), are making the biodynamic commitment, believing that soil health ultimately determines wine quality.
