OVERVIEW

Japan's Wine Story in Brief

Japanese wine has a history of over 150 years — older than California's modern wine industry, older than Australia's. Yet it remained largely invisible to the international wine world until the 2010s. The story of how it emerged is one of perseverance, government policy, wartime survival, and ultimately a quality revolution driven by a handful of passionate producers.

Japan's wine journey is inseparable from its modern history. The Meiji Restoration of 1868 launched a period of rapid Western-style modernization — and wine was part of the package. It was fashionable, Western, and potentially valuable as an export. Government-sponsored winery projects followed within a decade. What the early pioneers discovered, however, was that Japan's climate was profoundly hostile to European wine grapes. The long struggle to adapt — through vine selection, viticulture innovation, and ultimately the embrace of indigenous and hybrid varieties — defines the first century of Japanese wine.

The second century has been a story of quality. From the 1990s onward, a generation of producers focused on terroir-driven, internationally competitive wines. The GI system formalized this commitment to quality origin in 2013. By the 2020s, Japanese wine was winning awards at major international competitions, featured on Michelin three-star restaurant lists, and attracting collectors who pay substantial prices for limited releases from Hokkaido and Nagano.

MEIJI ERA (1868–1912)
1874
Japan's First Commercial Winery
The Meiji government establishes the Yamakashi Prefecture Grape Wine Brewing Company (山梨県葡萄酒醸造会社) at the foot of Mount Fuji in Kōfu. It is Japan's first systematic attempt at commercial winemaking. Early results are mixed — European Vitis vinifera varieties struggle with Japan's humid summers, and the local Koshu grape, long cultivated as a table fruit, produces inconsistent wine.
1877
Two Young Japanese Go to France
Masanao Tsuchiya (土屋正直) and Ryuken Takano (高野龍憲), both in their early twenties, are dispatched to France by the Yamanashi prefectural government to study winemaking. They spend two years learning viticulture and vinification at a time when Bordeaux's scientific wine industry was revolutionizing world winemaking. They return with knowledge that shapes Japanese winemaking for generations. The Daikoku Budoshu Co. (大黒葡萄酒), founded in the same year and widely considered the predecessor of modern Château Mercian, also begins production.
1880
Mercian's Predecessor Founded
The forerunner of Château Mercian — Japan's largest wine company — is established in Yamanashi. Over the following decades it will become central to Japanese wine production, commercialization, and research. The company's Kikyogahara Merlot, first produced in 1985, will become one of Japan's most internationally acclaimed wines.
1890s–1910s
Slow Growth, Many Failures
The Meiji era wine industry grows slowly and unevenly. European grape varieties repeatedly fail in Japan's humid conditions, leading producers to rely on hybrid varieties and Koshu. Wine quality is inconsistent; distribution is limited. Wine remains a niche, Westernized product for the educated urban class rather than a mass-market beverage. Beer, sake, and shochu dominate.
TAISHO & EARLY SHOWA (1912–1945)
1927
Zenbei Kawakami Creates Muscat Bailey A
At the Iwanohara Vineyard (岩の原葡萄園) in Joetsu, Niigata Prefecture, viticulturist Zenbei Kawakami (川上善兵衛) completes a 40-year breeding program by successfully crossing Bailey (a Triumph × Muscat Hamburg hybrid) with Muscat Hamburg to create Muscat Bailey A. The new variety is disease-resistant, early-ripening, and produces wines with a distinctly Japanese character — fresh, lightly tannic, and highly food-friendly. Kawakami develops more than 100 hybrid varieties during his career; MBA is his masterpiece, now Japan's most widely planted red wine grape.
1940s
Wartime Decline
World War II devastates Japan's nascent wine industry. Agricultural resources are redirected to food production; grapes are used for juice and non-alcoholic products. Winemaking equipment falls into disrepair. Several small wineries close permanently. The industry that survives the war is a skeleton of its pre-war self.
POSTWAR RECONSTRUCTION (1945–1975)
1950s
Cheap Wine Era Begins
Postwar reconstruction demands cheap calories, not quality wine. Japanese "wine" of this era is frequently made from imported grape concentrate diluted with water and sugar — a practice technically legal under existing regulations. Bottles bearing the word "wine" in Japan during this period often have little in common with vinified grape juice. This era establishes the negative quality associations that the genuine Japanese wine movement must overcome decades later.
1960s
Japan's Wine Boom & the Imported Concentrate Problem
Rising incomes and Western lifestyle aspirations create Japan's first mass-market wine boom in the 1960s. But growth in wine "production" is largely growth in the blending of imported grape concentrate with local additions — not genuine viticulture. Major companies like Suntory, Mercian, and Manns Wine begin investing in quality production, but their premium products coexist with a large-volume segment of dubious quality.
1972
Suntory Enters Winemaking
Suntory — already Japan's largest whisky and beer company — begins serious investment in wine, establishing vineyards in Yamanashi and later acquiring the Tomi no Oka Winery. Suntory's resources and R&D capabilities push quality Yamanashi Koshu and red wine production to new technical heights.
THE QUALITY REVOLUTION (1975–2010)
1975–1985
The First Quality Generation
A generation of producers begins asking: what can Japan do that Europe cannot? Rather than trying to replicate Bordeaux, they focus on Japan's unique resources — Koshu, the Yamanashi climate, and the specific flavors that Japanese consumers and Japan's extraordinary cuisine demand. Château Mercian's enologist team begins systematic Koshu experimentation. Grace Wine, established in 1923 as a co-operative but reinvented under quality-focused management, begins producing wines that catch the attention of Japan's new wine media.
1985
First Kikyogahara Merlot Vintage
Château Mercian produces the first vintage of its now-famous Kikyogahara Merlot from a hillside plot in Nagano's Shiojiri City. The wine's quality — structured, complex, and capable of aging — establishes that Japan can produce internationally competitive red wines. Subsequent vintages win medals at international competitions and appear on premium restaurant wine lists.
1990s
Imported Wine Dominates; Japanese Wine Fights Back
The 1990s Japanese wine boom is driven primarily by imported wines — Bordeaux, Burgundy, Californian Cabernet. Wine consumption per capita rises dramatically, but imported wine takes the premium segment. Japanese wine responds by tightening quality — reducing reliance on imported concentrate, investing in vineyards, and learning from the international wines now flooding Japanese restaurants. A label reform debate begins: consumers realize they cannot distinguish domestic wine made from Japanese grapes from "wine" made from imported concentrate. The label law reform will take two more decades.
1994–2000
Grace Wine's Quality Pivot
Grace Wine, under the leadership of the Misawa family (particularly enologist Misawa Shigekazu), pivots decisively to quality Koshu production. Their "Beau Michelle" Koshu — dry, mineral, food-forward — becomes the benchmark for the style. Grace Wine's wines begin winning international awards, and Misawa becomes one of Japan's most respected wine advocates internationally. The winery's success demonstrates that Koshu, properly vinified, can compete with the world's best white wines on quality terms.
2002
Japan Wine Producers Association Founded
The Japan Wine Producers Association (日本ワイナリーアワード協議会 and related bodies) is formally established, bringing together producers committed to quality Japanese wine. The association advocates for labeling reform, international recognition, and the promotion of Japanese varieties internationally. It plays a key role in the OIV applications for Koshu (2010) and Muscat Bailey A (2013).
2010
Koshu Recognized by the OIV
The OIV (International Organisation of Vine and Wine) formally registers Koshu as an international wine grape variety — a recognition that acknowledges its genetic distinctiveness and commercial significance. The registration (the first OIV acceptance of a Japanese grape variety) opens the door to Koshu's inclusion in international wine competitions on equal footing with Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. Three years later, Muscat Bailey A follows.
THE GI ERA & GLOBAL RECOGNITION (2013–PRESENT)
2013
GI Yamanashi — Japan's First Wine GI
Japan's National Tax Agency (NTA) designates GI Yamanashi as Japan's first wine Geographical Indication — a watershed moment equivalent to Bordeaux's AOC designation in 1936. GI Yamanashi wines must use at least 85% Yamanashi-grown grapes and be vinified within the prefecture. The designation provides the legal framework for authenticity that quality producers have been seeking for decades. Simultaneously, Muscat Bailey A is registered by the OIV as an international variety, the second Japanese grape to receive this recognition.
2015
Japanese Wine at Michelin Three-Star Level
Several of Tokyo's Michelin three-star restaurants begin featuring Japanese wine — particularly premium Koshu and Nagano reds — on their wine lists. This is a decisive cultural moment: Japanese wine achieves domestic prestige acceptance at the highest level of culinary culture. International sommeliers visiting Japan discover Grace Wine, Château Mercian Kikyogahara, and early Domaine Takahiko releases; social media amplification follows.
2018
Labeling Reform — "Japanese Wine" Defined
October 2018: Japan's revised liquor tax labeling standards take effect, requiring that wine labeled "Japanese wine" (日本ワイン) be made entirely from Japan-grown grapes and vinified in Japan. Wines made from imported grape concentrate — previously also sold as "domestic wine" — must now use different terminology (kokusanwain, domestic wine). The reform is transformative: consumers can now trust the "Japanese wine" label as a genuine origin guarantee. Quality producers immediately benefit from the distinction.
2018
GI Hokkaido Designated
GI Hokkaido is designated, recognizing the island's distinctive cool-climate wine identity. The same year, Domaine Takahiko's Yoichi Pinot Noir releases begin attracting serious international collector interest — the first Japanese wine to command significant prices on the secondary market. Hokkaido wine tourism begins growing rapidly.
2021
Three New GIs: Nagano, Yamagata, Osaka
Three additional wine GIs are designated in 2021: GI Nagano (formalizing the Kikyogahara Merlot tradition and Nagano's high-altitude identity), GI Yamagata (recognizing the continental-climate reds and Dewa33), and GI Osaka (formalizing urban wine production). Japan now has five wine GIs — a robust quality infrastructure comparable to many European wine-producing nations.
2020s
Natural Wine, Orange Koshu & the Next Generation
The 2020s bring a new generation of Japanese wine producers — younger, often trained abroad, and deeply connected to global natural wine culture. Orange Koshu (skin-contact fermentation) moves from a curiosity to a category, with serious producers including Marquis Winery and Haramo winning converts among natural wine enthusiasts. Pet-nat Koshu releases sell out on allocation. Japanese wine appears at major European natural wine fairs. The industry that began with a Meiji government initiative in 1874 is now a globally watched phenomenon.
Where Japan stands today. As of 2026, Japan's 432 registered wineries (per Terroir HUB WINE's database) produce approximately 20,000–25,000 kiloliters of domestic Japanese wine annually — less than 0.1% of global production. But quality per bottle is exceptionally high at the premium tier, and the country's wine culture is growing rapidly. Annual wine consumption in Japan exceeds 350,000 kiloliters, of which Japanese wine represents roughly 6–7%. The gap between domestic production and imported wine consumption represents an enormous opportunity — and a market that Japanese producers are energetically pursuing.
